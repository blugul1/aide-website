INTRODUCTION — Before You Say Yes

The invitations have gone out. In one house, someone is counting guests. In another, someone is asking the caterer, for the third time, whether the food will be enough. A mother is on the phone to a sister in another country, describing the colour of the stage. A father is quietly adding up costs on the back of an envelope and hoping nobody notices.

In a quieter room, two people are thinking about the same wedding, and thinking about something else entirely.

One is wondering whether the new house will feel like home. The other is wondering how much money will be left after the wedding is over. One is wondering how often their mother will visit. The other is wondering whether they will be expected to give up a job, a city, or a plan they have held for years.

Nobody has asked these questions yet. Not because they are unimportant. Because everybody is busy arranging the wedding.

This book is about the preparation that happens before the wedding, and it is not about flowers or seating plans. It is about the life that starts when the guests go home.

A wedding is an event. It has a date, a venue, a guest list, a budget, and a closing ceremony. A marriage has none of those things in the same way. It has mornings and evenings, bills and tired days, good news and bad news, parents who grow older, and plans that change. The wedding may last a day or several days. The marriage will occupy a much larger part of your life. And yet families can spend months discussing the stage while the two people getting married have never once discussed where they will live, how money will be handled, or what will happen when they disagree.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying a wedding. It is a blessing, a family occasion, and a moment worth marking well. A modest celebration can be beautiful. A large one can be beautiful too. The trouble begins when the celebration takes all the attention and the marriage gets whatever is left.

Marriage is not simply a match between two families

In South Asian communities, marriage rarely concerns only two individuals. Parents, siblings, grandparents, uncles, and aunties are part of it. They introduce people, offer advice, arrange meetings, and help with the wedding. This involvement can be a beautiful source of support. A family that cares for a new couple, helps them settle, and stands by them in difficulty is a gift.

But family involvement can also create assumptions, and assumptions are where trouble quietly begins. A son may expect his wife to live with his parents because that is how marriage has always worked in his family. A daughter may expect a separate home because that is what her friends have. A family in Pakistan may assume that a daughter moving to the UK will continue the same household arrangements she grew up with. A family in the UK may assume that a son-in-law from Bangladesh will fit easily into a life he has never lived.

Sometimes the differences are not religious at all. They are simply differences in upbringing. One home ate together every evening. Another ate whenever people were hungry. One home discussed money openly. Another considered it rude to ask. One home welcomed visitors at any hour. Another guarded its evenings. None of these homes was necessarily wrong. But two people who have never talked about them will be surprised by each other, and surprise in a new marriage can feel like disappointment if nobody names it in time.

A familiar background is not proof of compatibility. A different background is not proof of incompatibility either. What matters is whether two people can understand each other's expectations and decide, together, how they will live.

Why seven conversations?

There are hundreds of things worth discussing before marriage. This book brings many of the important ones together into seven conversations: money; home and in-laws; children and parenting; roles and expectations; the past, phones, and boundaries; faith and values; and fighting and forgiving.

These are not seven interviews, and they are certainly not seven tests. You do not need to sit opposite each other with a notebook and demand immediate answers. A conversation can happen on a walk, over chai, on a phone call, or in a car. It can be short. It can be unfinished. It can be returned to later. What you are looking for is where your understanding is shared, where it differs, and what you still need to find out.

Saying that you both want children is a beginning, not a complete discussion. How soon? What will change at work? Who will look after a small child? What kind of schooling do you hope for? Similarly, saying that you respect your parents does not settle the question of where you will live, how often they will visit, or who will decide about the household. The details matter because married life is made of details. It is made of who cooks when both of you are tired, who pays a bill that arrives unexpectedly, and who answers the phone when a relative calls with a strong opinion.

A word about Islamic marriage

Nikah is the Islamic marriage contract. Mahr is the obligatory marital gift that the husband owes his wife. These words carry more than ceremonial meaning. Marriage in Islam is a serious commitment involving rights, responsibilities, mercy, and good character. The Qur'an describes marriage in terms of affection and mercy (Qur'an 30:21), and that description should influence how a couple speaks to each other, especially when they disagree.

Discussing money is not a lack of trust. Asking about living arrangements is not disrespect towards parents. Asking about the past, where it affects the marriage, is not suspicion. A person can be religious and still need to learn how to communicate. A person can sincerely love their spouse and still need to learn how to manage a household. Religious values should help people deal with one another justly. They should not become a convenient way of avoiding a difficult conversation.

It is also important to distinguish between cultural practices and religious requirements. Mahr is a religious obligation. Many wedding customs are not. A family tradition is not automatically a religious duty, and a religious duty is not automatically a family tradition. When you can tell the difference, many arguments become simpler.

The world in which you will marry

A South Asian marriage today may begin in one country and continue in another. A young woman from Lahore may marry someone working in Dubai. Two British Pakistanis may be introduced through family and marry in the same city where they grew up. A man from Dhaka may move to the United States after his wedding. A woman from Birmingham may marry someone from Karachi and wait months for paperwork to be settled.

The geographical distance is only one part of the matter. There may be immigration applications, employment uncertainty, different education systems, rent or a mortgage, and family members living in three different time zones. A family may be physically distant but emotionally present every day through video calls and WhatsApp groups. A decision about a house, a job, or a child's schooling can involve opinions from people who have never lived in the country where the couple will live.

The question is not whether these arrangements are right or wrong. The question is whether both people understand them in the same way. If one person believes that moving abroad means immediate financial comfort and the other knows that the first year will be tight, that gap matters. If one person expects frequent visits from relatives and the other expects privacy in a small rented flat, that gap matters too. Silence does not create agreement. It merely postpones the discovery of disagreement, and postponed disagreement is usually more painful.

What this book asks of you

As you read, resist the temptation to turn every question towards the other person. Ask yourself what you expect from marriage. What can you reasonably offer? Which responsibilities are you ready to take on, and which ones frighten you? Then listen to the other person. Do not listen merely to prepare your reply. Listen to understand what their answer means for the life you will share.

You may discover that you agree on most matters. You may find a few important differences. Some will be easy to settle. Some will need time, advice, or further discussion. That is useful information. It is better to discover it before making a lifelong commitment than to discover it afterwards, when both of you are already tired and disappointed.

One thing must be said plainly, and it stands apart from everything else in this book: if there is real abuse or coercion, that is not an ordinary difference to be talked through — safety comes first, and qualified help should be sought at once.

For everyone else, the central invitation of this book is simple: approach marriage with sincerity, patience, and honesty. You do not need to become experts in marriage before your nikah. You do need to become more honest about what you want, what you can give, and what you still need to learn. The person sitting across from you is not a project to be corrected. Nor are you entering marriage to be corrected yourself. You are two people deciding whether you can build a life together, with your families, your faith, and your circumstances as they really are.

Before the wedding invitations are printed, before the suit is collected, before the photographer asks you to smile, put the wedding arrangements aside for an evening. Talk about the home you will return to when the celebration is over.

Practical step: Arrange one uninterrupted conversation with your prospective spouse. Do not begin with all seven topics. Begin with one: what does a good married life look like to you, in an ordinary week? Each of you should speak for ten minutes without interruption, then repeat back what you heard before replying.

Readiness check

  1. Can I explain what I personally expect from married life, rather than simply repeating what my family expects?
  2. Am I prepared to hear an answer that differs from my own, without treating it as a rejection?
  3. Are we willing to discuss important matters before making a final commitment, even when the conversation is uncomfortable?

CHAPTER 1 — A Wedding Is an Event, a Marriage Is a System

The first morning after the wedding, the house is quieter than it has been for weeks. The clothes from the previous evening are hanging over a chair. There are flowers on the table, a few plates that nobody has washed, and a suitcase still half unpacked by the door.

The husband is looking for his office keys. His wife is wondering whether breakfast will be made before he leaves. He assumes she knows his working hours. She assumes he will stay for a while. His mother calls from downstairs to ask whether they would like parathas. His wife, who grew up in a house where breakfast was a quiet affair, is not sure whether to go down, or wait, or offer to help.

Nobody has done anything particularly wrong. But three expectations have collided before the first cup of tea has been finished. This is married life beginning. The wedding was carefully arranged. The morning after it was not. That difference deserves our attention.

The wedding has a plan. Does the marriage?

A wedding is relatively easy to organise because its requirements are visible. There is a venue to book, clothing to arrange, guests to invite, food to prepare, and a budget to manage. You can make a list, tick items off, and see the day take shape. Even when something goes wrong, there is usually someone to call.

A marriage is different. There is no final guest list for married life. No one hands you a programme explaining who will manage the money, who will cook on a tired evening, who will answer a difficult phone call from a relative, or how you will decide when you disagree. The marriage has to develop its own working arrangements, and those arrangements have to keep working when life changes around them.

Think of a household as a small system. It contains people, money, time, responsibilities, relationships, and expectations, all connected to each other. If one person loses a job, the household budget changes. If a parent becomes unwell, family responsibilities change. If a couple moves to another country, almost everything changes at once — the home, the income, the support around them, and the daily routine. A marriage does not operate in isolation from the world around it. It has to function within that world.

This is why affection, although essential, cannot answer every practical question. Two people may love each other and still disagree about where to live. They may share religious beliefs and still manage money differently. They may both respect their parents and still disagree about how much time the family spends together. Love gives people a reason to make an effort. It does not automatically provide the arrangements that effort needs.

The problem with assuming that everything will work out

There is a familiar sentence in many marriage discussions: "We will understand each other after marriage." There is some truth in it. Marriage does reveal things that cannot be fully understood beforehand. Living together teaches you things that no conversation can teach. But this sentence can also become an excuse for avoiding questions that could easily be discussed now, while there is still time to think clearly.

If someone asks where you intend to live, saying "We will see after nikah" is not a plan. If someone asks whether you expect your parents to live with you, saying "They are my parents; I will look after them" is a good intention, not an arrangement. If someone asks how you will manage money, saying "Allah will provide" expresses faith, but it does not answer the question. Trust in Allah does not mean abandoning responsible planning. We make reasonable efforts, consider our circumstances, seek advice where needed, and place our trust in Allah alongside that effort, not instead of it. In fact, practical preparation can be an expression of responsibility, and of care for the person you are marrying.

Two people bring two different homes into one marriage

Every person arrives at marriage carrying a household history. One may have grown up in a home where everyone ate together. The other may have grown up with family members eating at different times because of work. One may consider it natural for parents to have a key to the house. The other may believe that visits should be arranged in advance. One may have watched their father manage all the finances. The other may have grown up in a household where both parents worked and shared the bills. One family may express affection openly. Another may show care through practical help and quiet sacrifice.

Neither household necessarily had the perfect arrangement. The difficulty begins when each person assumes that their own childhood experience represents the normal way of doing things. A husband may say, "In our house, we always do it this way." His wife may quietly think, "That is how your house worked. This is our house now." The same disagreement can occur in reverse. A wife may expect regular family gatherings because they were central to her upbringing. Her husband may find large gatherings tiring after a long week at work.

Think of it instead as the chance to create a third household: one that respects the good in both backgrounds while leaving aside what does not work for this couple, in this place, at this time. A marriage should not require one person to disappear into the other's family culture. Nor should establishing a new household require either person to reject their parents. The task is more thoughtful than that. It asks a couple to choose, together, what their home will be like — and to keep choosing, as life changes.

The diaspora dimension: where is home?

For many South Asian couples living abroad, the question of home is more complicated than choosing an address. A young man may live with his parents in Birmingham. He is working, contributing to household expenses, and comfortable in the family home. When marriage is discussed, he assumes that she will move into the family home. She assumes they will rent a flat together. Both have been speaking sincerely about marriage. Neither has been dishonest. They have simply been imagining different homes.

Now introduce the practical details. The family home may have limited space. The couple may have different working schedules. The parents may be used to a busy house, with relatives dropping in. The new wife may be used to quiet evenings. There may be no easy answer. But there is a very important question: what home are we actually agreeing to establish?

For one couple, the answer may be living with parents for an agreed period while saving for a separate home. For another, it may be renting nearby so that parents are close but the couple has its own space. For another, it may be moving to a different city, or a different country, for work. The answer should come from informed agreement, not from an assumption made by one side. And the discussion should not stop at the address. Ask about how much privacy the couple will have; whether they will have their own bedroom, bathroom, and living space; who will manage cooking and household work; how expenses will be shared; who will make decisions about visitors; what happens when relatives visit from Pakistan, India, or Bangladesh for several weeks; and if the arrangement becomes difficult, how it will be reviewed.

A couple living separately may still face a similar issue. Parents may live fifteen minutes away. They may expect frequent visits. A sibling may need temporary accommodation. A parent may become unwell and need daily help. Physical distance does not automatically establish emotional or practical boundaries. On the other hand, living together does not automatically mean a marriage lacks privacy. The quality of the arrangement depends on its details, and on whether the people involved understand those details in the same way.

Family involvement: respect without surrendering every decision

South Asian families are often connected through constant communication. There may be one WhatsApp group for the immediate family, another for cousins, another for the wider family, and a separate stream of messages from relatives overseas. A photograph of a newly decorated room may receive twenty suggestions. A decision about visiting parents may be discussed by a dozen people before the couple has discussed it with each other. An overseas relative may have strong opinions about how the couple should spend money, educate children, or arrange their home, even though that relative has never lived in the country where the couple lives.

Sometimes this involvement is helpful. A relative may know a good doctor, help with immigration paperwork, or offer a place to stay. Sometimes the amount of involvement becomes exhausting. A useful distinction is between receiving advice and transferring authority. You can listen to your parents without making them responsible for every marital decision. You can seek a relative's opinion without handing them the decision itself. The husband and wife should be able to discuss their disagreements privately before inviting others into them. This does not mean concealing important matters or disrespecting parents. It means recognising that a marriage needs a private space in which the couple can think, disagree, and decide.

There is another practical point worth discussing before nikah: how will each spouse handle private matters with their own family? Will arguments be reported immediately to parents? Will screenshots of private messages be forwarded to a sibling or a cousin? Will a disagreement be discussed in a family group before it has been discussed at home? If either person expects that level of family involvement, it is better to discuss it openly now than to discover it during the first serious argument.

A household runs on money, time, and labour

People sometimes discuss marriage as though the only important financial question is how much someone earns. Income matters, but it is only one part of household functioning. A person may earn well and still have significant debt. Another may earn less but have more manageable expenses. A couple may face rent, utilities, transport, insurance, childcare, education costs, taxes, and obligations to parents — all at the same time, and sometimes in more than one currency.

There may also be a question of how mahr will be agreed and fulfilled, and whether either family expects jahez, the gifts and household items a bride's family may provide. These matters belong in the money conversation, and they belong there before the nikah, when they can be discussed calmly. They should not be left until after the wedding, when embarrassment and family pressure make honest discussion much harder.

But money is not the only resource a household needs. Time is also a resource. Who will cook after a ten-hour working day? Who will arrange appointments, manage school runs, or deal with a landlord? And what about mental effort — remembering that a bill is due, arranging a relative's visit, buying groceries before the weekend, or noticing that a parent needs help? If one spouse is expected to notice and manage everything while the other merely helps when asked, resentment will grow quietly, even between people who love each other. A household needs agreed responsibilities, not an unspoken assumption that one person will carry the whole weight of it. This is especially important when both spouses have careers. A couple may each have a full-time job but discover that only one of them is expected to adjust working hours, take time off, or give up opportunities when family needs arise. That arrangement deserves an honest discussion before it becomes a pattern.

The danger of a marriage built around appearances

South Asian families can invest enormous energy in presenting a good wedding. There is pressure to look respectable, arrange a memorable event, and meet family expectations. Sometimes a couple begins married life with financial commitments that were never really necessary. The wedding photographs will remain. The repayments will also remain. There is no virtue in beginning a marriage under financial pressure simply to satisfy an audience.

A modest walima — the wedding meal hosted after the nikah — can be a perfectly meaningful beginning, and so can a rented flat. A small wedding is not a small marriage. What matters is whether the couple can establish a stable, responsible household within their real circumstances. A wedding is visible. Much of marriage is not. The world sees the clothes, the photographs, the venue, and the celebration. It does not see the private conversations, the apologies, the compromises, or the quiet work of building a home. That unseen work is where much of married life happens.

The marriage needs agreements, but it also needs flexibility

It would be a mistake to turn this book into a demand that every detail of married life must be decided before the wedding. That is impossible. People change. Jobs change. Countries change. Parents grow older. Children may arrive. Health, finances, and opportunities shift in ways nobody can predict. A couple cannot write a complete manual for a future they have not yet lived.

What they can do is establish a way of discussing change. If a couple agrees to live with parents for the first year, they can also agree to review that arrangement after a year. If one spouse plans to continue studying, they can discuss what support will be required and how household work will be shared during that period. If a couple expects to move abroad, they can discuss what they know, what remains uncertain, and what they will do if the move is delayed. An agreement is not a prison. It is a shared understanding that can be reviewed when circumstances change. There is a difference between changing an arrangement after a genuine discussion and repeatedly ignoring it. Trust grows when both people take their commitments seriously, and when both are willing to revisit them honestly.

Do not marry an imaginary version of each other

One of the most common mistakes in preparing for marriage is falling in love with an assumption. A person thinks, "Once we are married, he will become more responsible." Another thinks, "After nikah, she will understand my family." Someone assumes a spouse will become more religious. Someone else assumes a spouse will become less strict. These expectations may be based on hope rather than evidence, and hope is not a plan.

It is not fair to marry someone and then treat the marriage as a project to transform them into the person you wanted. People can grow. They can learn. They can change their habits. But change requires willingness, effort, and time, and it cannot be demanded as a condition of love. Before marriage, pay attention to what the other person actually says and does. If they repeatedly avoid discussing money, do not assume that financial transparency will appear automatically after the wedding. If they insist that their parents will make every major household decision, do not assume that this will change. If they expect you to give up an important career or personal commitment, ask what that expectation really means, and whether you can live with it.

You are not required to agree with every expectation. You are required to understand what you are being asked to agree to. And the same standard applies to you. Do not ask for openness while concealing your own expectations. Do not demand flexibility while refusing to offer any. Marriage is not a negotiation in which one person must win. It is a decision about whether two people can build a workable life together.

Seven conversations, one shared responsibility

The seven conversations in this book are connected. Money affects where you live. Where you live affects your relationship with parents. Family arrangements affect how children are raised. Expectations about roles affect how money and time are shared. A disagreement about one thing is rarely only about that one thing.

Imagine a couple disagreeing about whether the husband's parents should move into their home. At first, the issue appears to belong to home and in-laws. But the discussion may also involve money, privacy, parenting, religious responsibilities, expectations about who cares for elderly parents, and how decisions are made. This is why it helps to discuss the subjects separately before trying to resolve them together. Not every conversation will produce a final decision. Some will reveal questions that require further discussion, or advice from a qualified person. That is not wasted time. A difficult conversation before marriage is not necessarily a sign that the match is wrong. Equally, an easy conversation is not proof that everything is settled. The quality of a conversation matters more than how quickly it ends.

Try to leave each discussion knowing three things: what do we agree on, where do we differ, and what do we need to find out before deciding? If the answer to the third question is that you need legal advice, religious guidance, or financial information, then finding that out is part of the preparation, not a delay to it.

Begin with the life, not the ceremony

There is a small but important exercise I want you to try. Imagine an ordinary Tuesday, six months after your wedding. No guests. No photographs. No special celebration. You both wake up. One has work. The other may have work, study, or household responsibilities. There is washing to do, food to prepare, and a day to get through.

Now describe that day to each other. What time would you like to wake up? Would you prefer to eat together? How much time would you like to spend with family in the evening? Who will cook? Who will clean? What will you do when you are both tired and the house still needs attention?

Do not laugh off the answers. A person who wants a quiet evening after work may be marrying someone who considers evening family visits essential. Neither needs to be labelled difficult. But both need to understand the difference. You may find that your imagined Tuesday is surprisingly similar. You may discover that you have been imagining different lives. Either way, you have learned something worth knowing.

A wedding can be arranged around a date. A marriage has to be built around two real people, their families, their responsibilities, and the life they will actually live. Before you spend another evening discussing the wedding stage, spend some time discussing the home that will be waiting when the music stops.

Practical step: Each person should independently write a description of an ordinary weekday during the first year of marriage — waking time, work or study, meals, household work, family time, and rest. Then read each other's description together, note where they match and where they differ, and discuss one difference without trying to settle it in that same sitting.

Readiness check

  1. Can we describe the kind of household we intend to establish, including where and with whom we expect to live?
  2. Have we discussed the influence of our families without assuming that either person must abandon their parents or surrender every decision to them?
  3. Are we prepared to revise our expectations when practical circumstances require it, without treating a change of plan as a broken promise?

CONVERSATION 1 — MONEY: WHAT ARE WE BUILDING TOGETHER?

The restaurant bill arrives. The young man reaches for his wallet. His prospective wife offers to contribute. He smiles and says there is no need. She puts her purse away. On the drive home, neither of them mentions the things that sit quietly behind that small moment. He sends money to his parents every month. She is still repaying a student loan. His family expects a generous wedding. Her family has been saving for years. Both of them consider themselves financially responsible. Both of them are also preparing to enter a financial relationship whose actual shape they have never discussed.

Money is the conversation couples avoid most. It can feel uncomfortable, especially when families are involved, and it can feel as though asking about money means doubting the other person. So the subject is postponed. Then marriage begins, and the questions arrive all at once: rent, groceries, bills, family contributions, debts, savings, and the cost of a wedding that has already been paid for. It would have been much easier to discuss them over tea before the nikah, when there was still time to think clearly and no bill was already overdue.

How to open this talk

If you do not know how to begin, try this line, in your own words: "I would like us to understand each other's financial responsibilities before marriage. I am not asking because I doubt you. I am asking because I want us to begin honestly." That is enough to open the door. You do not need to turn the first conversation into an audit. Begin with the intention of understanding, and let the details follow. Choose a calm time, not the evening before a wedding event, and not in the middle of an argument about something else. If the conversation becomes tense, pause it. A paused conversation can be resumed. A conversation that turns into a quarrel leaves both people less willing to return to it.

It helps to say, early on, what money means to you. For one person, money means security, and a healthy savings balance helps them sleep at night. For another, money means generosity, and being able to help parents or host guests matters more than a large balance. For a third, money means freedom, the ability to choose a job or a city without being trapped by debt. None of these views is wrong on its own. Trouble starts when two people hold different views and never name them, so each assumes the other shares their view and is then surprised, or disappointed, when they do not.

Income is not the whole financial picture

A salary tells you how much money comes in. It does not tell you how much remains available after the month's commitments have been met. Someone earning a good monthly income may have substantial loan repayments, high housing costs, or regular family contributions. The same salary can support very different lifestyles in different cities and countries. A couple living in Birmingham may face a different housing budget from a couple living in Dubai, and a household in Houston will have different costs again. Do not compare your financial circumstances with relatives, friends, or photographs on social media. Understand your own position first, and discuss it together.

Each person should be able to explain, in plain terms, their current income and whether it is regular or variable; their employment situation and any likely changes; their existing savings; their debts, loans, and repayment commitments; their regular family contributions; and any financial obligations that may continue after marriage. If one person is self-employed, works on contracts, or receives irregular income, explain how that affects the household in a slow month. If one person is studying, between jobs, or dependent on family support, say so clearly. There is no shame in having limited income. Concealing a financial commitment that will affect married life is a different matter, and it is the concealment, not the amount, that usually causes the deeper hurt.

Debt: the question that needs an honest answer

Debt can arise for many reasons. Education, housing, business, medical expenses, and ordinary living costs can all leave a person with repayments. The important matter before marriage is not to judge someone merely for having borrowed money. It is to understand what is owed, to whom, and for how long, so that the couple can plan around it. Ask what debts or loans exist, what the outstanding amount is, what the regular repayment is, when repayment is expected to finish, whether the amount is fixed or variable, whether anyone else is legally responsible for it, whether you have guaranteed someone else's loan, and whether any financial commitment could become payable unexpectedly.

If a person has a mortgage, a student loan, a credit-card balance, or business borrowing, the other person deserves to know before marriage, not after the first joint bill arrives. Where interest-based borrowing is involved, the couple may also wish to seek qualified Islamic financial guidance, because the religious questions here are real and should not be settled by guesswork or by a family opinion offered in passing. Do not assume that marriage automatically makes one spouse responsible for the other's personal debt, and do not make promises about clearing debt after marriage unless there is a realistic plan behind the promise. A hopeful sentence is not a repayment plan.

Parents, remittances, and family responsibilities

In many South Asian households, financial responsibility extends beyond the immediate couple. A son may send money to his parents in Pakistan while working in the Gulf. A daughter may help her widowed mother with household expenses. A sibling may be studying, and the family may be supporting them together. These commitments deserve respectful discussion, not silence and not resentment. Supporting parents can be an important expression of gratitude and responsibility. The question is how much, how regularly, and how it fits with the needs of the new household.

Suppose one person sends a regular amount to their parents. The other should understand the approximate amount, whether it is fixed or flexible, and whether it is likely to increase when circumstances require it. What happens if a parent needs additional support, or if the couple is saving for a home at the same time? It helps to distinguish between a regular commitment and an occasional emergency, and to agree how emergencies will be handled. Neither spouse should discover after marriage that a substantial part of the household income is already committed elsewhere. At the same time, a discussion about family support should not become an attempt to isolate someone from their parents. The aim is a realistic financial picture that respects both the new household and existing responsibilities, and that both people can live with.

Mahr: agree before the nikah

Mahr is the marital gift owed by the husband to the wife. It belongs to the wife, and it should be discussed plainly, agreed freely, and recorded appropriately in the nikah documentation. Do not leave the matter entirely to assumptions between families, and do not leave it until the day of the nikah, when everyone is busy and nobody wants to raise an awkward question. A mahr may be paid immediately, deferred, or divided into agreed portions, depending on the arrangement the couple makes. If instalments are agreed, specify the total agreed amount, what is payable immediately, what is deferred, the amount and timing of any instalments, the circumstances and date by which deferred payment becomes due, and how the agreement will be recorded.

If the mahr is denominated in a currency different from the one in which payment will be made, clarify that too, because exchange rates and transfer costs can change what a figure actually means. Avoid choosing an impressive figure merely to satisfy relatives. Equally, do not pressure someone to accept a token figure if they have a different expectation. A promise is meaningful when the person making it understands the obligation and intends to fulfil it. Mahr is not a decorative figure written on a document. It is a real responsibility, and treating it plainly before marriage is a sign of respect, not a lack of romance. For religious questions about the validity or detailed terms of an arrangement, consult a qualified scholar.

Jahez: separate custom from obligation

The word jahez is commonly used for goods, money, or household items provided by the bride's family in connection with a wedding. Practices differ across families and countries. So do legal rules. Do not assume that the laws of Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, Britain, America, or a Gulf country are identical, and do not assume that what happened at a cousin's wedding settles what should happen at yours. The religious distinction matters here. Mahr is the husband's obligation to the wife. Jahez is not a religious obligation in the same sense, and it should never be treated as a price for marriage or as a test of a family's standing.

If the bride's family freely wishes to provide gifts, the couple can discuss what is useful and appropriate, and what would simply add pressure. Ask each other directly whether either family is expecting particular gifts, whether anyone expects the bride's family to pay for household furnishings, whether these gifts are genuinely voluntary, who will own the items, and whether any expectation could create financial pressure or conflict. It is better to have a modest and clear arrangement than an expensive one that begins married life with debt or resentment. If a family is under pressure to provide more than it can afford, that pressure deserves to be named before the wedding, not discovered afterwards in the form of loans that take years to repay.

Build a household budget together

Think of a budget as a way of seeing what is possible, and of noticing problems while they are still small. Before marriage, prepare a first estimate. Use the figures you actually know. If you do not know a figure, write that down instead of inventing one. The table below is a working sheet. Both people should complete it together and revise it when they have reliable information. The agreed amount is your planned figure. Actual is what you eventually spend or receive. Notes explain the figure, its timing, or any condition attached to it. You can add further rows for insurance, education, childcare, taxes, medical expenses, and other relevant costs. Filling every box does not finish the job. The table becomes useful when both people understand it and can live with it.

Joint money or separate money?

Some couples prefer one shared account for household expenses. Others keep individual accounts and contribute a fixed amount each. Some use a shared account for household costs and keep separate accounts for personal spending. There is no single arrangement suitable for every household. Discuss what will work in your circumstances. Will both salaries enter one account? Will each person contribute a fixed amount? Will contributions change if one income rises or falls? If one spouse earns significantly more, does that change how expenses are shared? If one spouse temporarily stops working to study, care for a child, or support a family member, what happens then?

The couple should also understand the distinction between financial ownership and household cooperation. Sharing household expenses does not erase the wife's right to her mahr or to property that belongs to her, and earning more does not give one spouse a right to control the other's personal spending without discussion. Islamic financial responsibilities should not be casually replaced with assumptions borrowed from another culture or from a television drama. Discuss the religious position with a qualified scholar if either person is uncertain, and write down what you have agreed so that neither person has to rely on memory alone when a disagreement arises later.

Spending, saving, and lifestyle

People have different relationships with money, and those differences usually come from childhood. One person may feel secure only when there is a healthy savings balance. Another may consider spending on family and guests a normal part of life. One may compare prices carefully. The other may prefer convenience and be willing to pay for it. These differences are manageable when discussed openly, and they become painful when they are discovered through arguments about individual purchases.

Talk about the lifestyle you can afford. Include eating out, holidays, clothing, gifts, subscriptions, and the cost of visiting family. If one person enjoys sending generous gifts to relatives, discuss the budget for those gifts. If the other prefers to save for a home, discuss that too. A couple should be able to distinguish between necessities, commitments, personal preferences, and occasional treats, and to agree how much each category can reasonably receive. Savings deserve a place in the discussion. What are you saving for — an emergency fund, a home, education, or a future move? How much can realistically be set aside each month? If the couple is considering property ownership, discuss the deposit, financing, maintenance, legal costs, and the risk of buying before either person is ready.

Financial surprises after marriage

Life does not always follow the budget. Employment can change. A business can have a difficult period. A family member may need help. A car can break down. A visa application can take longer than expected and cost more than planned. Discuss how you will respond when something unexpected happens. Will both spouses review the budget together? At what level of spending should a purchase be discussed first? A useful household habit is a short monthly financial conversation: look at what came in, what went out, what is still owed, and what needs to change. Keep the conversation factual. A higher electricity bill is a problem to investigate, not an opportunity to blame each other for leaving lights on.

If a financial surprise reveals a commitment that was not disclosed before marriage, do not let the discovery become the whole story. Ask what happened, what it means for the household now, and what needs to change so that it does not happen again. A marriage can recover from a financial mistake more easily than it can recover from a pattern of concealment. Honesty after a mistake matters, and so does a practical plan for dealing with the consequences. The couple that can talk about money calmly, even when the news is unwelcome, has a better chance of managing the next surprise together.

Money is a responsibility, not a measure of human worth

Your income does not determine your value as a spouse. A person who earns more is not automatically entitled to greater respect or unrestricted authority in the home. What matters is honesty, fairness, fulfilment of responsibilities, and a willingness to plan within real circumstances. Do not use money to humiliate, control, or silence one another. Do not conceal spending that materially affects the household. Remember what this discussion is really for: understanding whether the two of you can make sensible financial decisions together, in good times and in difficult ones.

There may be financial differences that require compromise. There may be commitments that make the path harder for a while. A good beginning does not require a perfect financial position. It requires a truthful one, and a willingness to keep returning to the table together as circumstances change. If you can discuss money before marriage without turning it into a test of worth, you have already practised something married life will ask of you again and again.

Practical step: Set aside one hour and complete the budget table above together. Each person should bring accurate information about income, debts, and regular commitments, and should write down every figure they do not yet know so it can be found before the next conversation. End the hour by agreeing one figure you will both keep to for the next month, even if it is small.

Readiness check

  1. What have we actually told each other about money that we had not said before this conversation?
  2. Which money question are we still avoiding, and what makes it hard to raise?
  3. What is our next concrete step — a figure to find, a person to ask, or a date to sit down again?

CONVERSATION 2 — HOME & IN-LAWS: WHERE WILL WE LIVE?

The young couple are sitting in the living room of his parents' house. His mother has made tea. His father is discussing the journey they will take after the wedding. A younger sibling is showing the prospective bride the room that would be hers. The young woman smiles and thanks everyone. On the drive home, her fiancé asks whether she liked the house. She says it is lovely. He asks whether she will be comfortable living there. She hesitates. She thought they would rent a small flat after marriage. He thought she knew the plan. Neither had considered the question sufficiently important to ask before. Yet where you live affects your privacy, your finances, your daily routine, your relationship with parents, your work, your friendships, and your sense of belonging. It deserves more than a casual conversation during wedding preparations.

The first question is not whether separate living is right or wrong

In some South Asian families, living with parents after marriage is the expected arrangement. In others, a separate home is expected from the start. In many diaspora families, the answer is somewhere in between: living with parents for a period, living nearby, or living separately while remaining closely involved. Both arrangements can work. Both can become difficult. A joint household may provide companionship, practical support, shared expenses, and help when parents grow older. A separate home may give a couple greater control over routines and privacy, and it may also bring higher costs, more housework, and less immediate help.

The question should be approached through actual capacity, not through a slogan. Can the couple afford a separate home without creating unreasonable financial pressure? Is there sufficient space for privacy in the family home? What are the working hours of everyone involved? What care do parents need now, and what might they need soon? These questions matter more than a general statement about what a good husband or wife should do. A decision that sounds virtuous in principle can still be unworkable in practice, and a decision that sounds selfish in principle can sometimes be the only arrangement the couple can sustain.

Begin with the actual housing situation

Suppose the husband is working in Manchester and currently lives with his parents. The family home has three bedrooms. The prospective wife works nearby. She expects a separate home, but rents have increased and the couple would need to save for a deposit. The husband is concerned about paying rent while also helping his parents. This is not a question that can be answered by saying, "A wife should adjust," or "Every married couple should live separately." They need to examine the circumstances, and to do so together.

Start with space. Is there a suitable bedroom? Is there a separate bathroom? Is there a place where the couple can spend time alone? A house may be physically large but still offer little privacy. Then consider financial capacity. What would a separate home cost in the area? Include rent or mortgage, utilities, council or municipal charges, transport, and insurance. Would a separate home require the couple to abandon essential savings or take on debt they cannot comfortably repay? Then consider working hours. A person working night shifts may need quiet during the day. Someone working from home may need uninterrupted time. Then consider family responsibilities. Are the parents elderly or dependent? Is there an established responsibility for regular care? Are siblings also contributing? Most importantly, does each prospective spouse understand the proposed arrangement and accept it freely? A decision made by one person and announced to the other is not a shared household plan.

Living with parents: discuss the details before moving in

If the couple is considering a joint household, do not stop at the statement that everyone gets along well. Good relationships are valuable, but a shared home involves practical arrangements that need to be discussed in advance. Consider privacy first. Will the couple have a room that is treated as their private space? Will family members knock before entering? Will the couple be able to close a door without it being treated as rudeness? Then consider bathrooms and shared facilities. How many people will share them? Are there different routines for morning preparation? Is there sufficient hot water, storage, and time for everyone to get ready for work?

Consider meals. Will everyone eat together? Who will cook? Will the couple contribute towards groceries? What happens when the couple want to eat at a different time, or invite a friend? Consider household work. Who cleans shared areas? Who manages laundry? Is one person expected to serve everyone because of their position in the family? Consider guests. Can relatives arrive without advance notice? Will overnight visitors use the couple's space? How will visits from overseas relatives, which may last for weeks, be managed? Consider expenses. Will the couple pay a fixed household contribution? Will they purchase their own groceries? How will bills be divided? And consider decision-making. Which matters belong to the couple, and which are genuinely shared family decisions? These questions may seem unusually detailed before marriage. They become much more important when people are living together every day.

There is also a need to discuss what happens if the arrangement does not work as expected. Perhaps the couple agrees to live with parents initially while saving for a separate home. That is a reasonable plan, but only if everyone understands what "initially" means. Six months? A year? Until a certain amount has been saved? An open-ended promise can create disappointment when each person has understood it differently. Agree a review date now, and write it down. A review date does not mean the arrangement is expected to fail. It means the couple and the family are willing to look honestly at how it is working.

Separate living: independence has a price

Some couples assume that a separate home will solve every family difficulty. It will not. A couple renting a flat may enjoy privacy but still face disagreements about family visits, financial support, and how much time is spent with each set of parents. They may also experience the practical pressures of running a household without nearby assistance: cooking, cleaning, repairs, and childcare all fall on two people, and there is no one downstairs to help when someone is unwell. Consider the full cost before deciding. Can the couple manage the rent or mortgage? Will they need a second car? How far will they live from parents, work, and the mosque? Will they be able to save anything, or will housing consume almost everything they earn?

A separate home should be a workable arrangement, not a status symbol. If living separately means spending nearly all available income on housing, working excessive hours, and having no time together, the privacy gained may be outweighed by the strain created. Equally, financial difficulty should not become an excuse to ignore the need for reasonable privacy. The aim is to find a sustainable arrangement. A couple may begin in a modest rented home and save gradually. Another may choose to live near parents, close enough to help and to be helped, but with a front door of their own. The details matter, and they matter more than the label attached to the arrangement.

The husband as a bridge, not a messenger

In many South Asian families, the husband becomes the main point of communication between his parents and his wife. This is a significant responsibility, and it deserves careful thought before marriage. There is a difference between helping two sides understand one another and carrying complaints from one side to the other. Imagine his mother says, "Your wife spends too much time in her room." He returns to his wife and repeats the complaint, word for word. His wife responds, "Your mother comments on everything I do." He carries that response back to his mother. Soon he has become a messenger for two people who may not have understood each other's intentions at all. Every conversation produces another complaint, and every complaint makes the next conversation harder.

A husband should try to be a bridge. That means listening carefully, understanding the actual issue, and addressing it in the right place. If his parents have a concern, he can discuss it with them directly and calmly, without presenting his wife as the cause of the difficulty. If his wife has a concern, he can listen to her fully before deciding what, if anything, needs to be said to his parents. He should not present his wife as the person who is preventing him from fulfilling his responsibilities, and he should not use his parents' wishes to avoid making decisions that belong to him and his wife. A bridge connects people. A messenger simply transports words, and words carried in anger usually arrive heavier than they left.

The wife also has a responsibility to communicate respectfully. If she has a concern about her husband's parents, she can raise it with her husband first, clearly and without exaggeration, and give him a chance to respond. Both spouses need to understand that the relationship with parents continues after marriage. Marriage does not require a man to choose between his wife and his parents, and it does not require a woman to choose between her husband and hers. It requires both people to learn how to hold those relationships together without letting one of them become a channel for complaints about the other.

A wife should not have to compete with her husband's family

A woman entering a new family may feel that she is expected to prove herself. She may be learning unfamiliar household routines, understanding family relationships, and adjusting to a home where everyone else already knows the rules. Her husband may have known his parents and siblings for his entire life. She may have known them only through a handful of visits. Expecting both sides to feel equally familiar immediately is unrealistic. Give the relationship time.

A wife should be able to build her own relationship with her in-laws. It does not need to be a copy of her husband's relationship with them. She may enjoy cooking with her mother-in-law. She may prefer to maintain a little distance. She may become close to a sister-in-law and find another relative harder to know. Respect should not be measured by how much of her personal life she gives up, and a family's warmth should not be measured by how quickly she adopts every household habit. Likewise, parents should not be expected to change every established habit immediately because their son has married. The couple should be thoughtful about how they introduce new routines, and patient with the adjustment on both sides.

A newly married woman may move into a household where everyone already has established habits, and she may feel that she must ask permission before using the kitchen, changing a routine, or inviting a friend. If this happens, the husband should pay attention. A home needs to become a place where both spouses can live with dignity. It should not feel as though one of them is a guest who must constantly seek approval. That does not mean the wife can disregard shared household arrangements. Everyone living in the home needs to respect common spaces and routines. It means the couple should discuss how much freedom and privacy they will have, and how ordinary decisions will be made. If the kitchen is shared, how will cooking times be managed? If family members regularly visit, how will the couple's time together be protected? A person should not have to negotiate every ordinary household activity as though they were visiting someone else's home.

The wife and her own family

The discussion must also include the wife's parents and relatives. Will she visit her family regularly? How will the couple divide holidays and family occasions? What happens when her parents need help, or when her family expects her to attend a gathering? These arrangements should not be treated as secondary simply because she has entered another family. A husband's relationship with his parents deserves respect. So does a wife's relationship with hers. Couples sometimes discuss how the wife will adjust to the husband's family but never ask how the husband will adjust to hers, or how much time, money, and attention will be given to both families. A balanced conversation includes both, and it includes the practical details: travel time, travel cost, who will drive, and what happens when both families want the couple on the same day.

Family WhatsApp groups and the problem of forwarded conversations

Modern family relationships have acquired a new feature: the screenshot. A disagreement begins privately. Someone takes a screenshot. It is forwarded to a sibling. The sibling forwards it to a parent. Within hours, a private exchange may become a family subject, and people who were not part of the original conversation now have opinions about it. Even when no one intends harm, this can damage trust, because the couple no longer feels safe discussing difficult matters without an audience.

Discuss your expectations about digital communication before marriage. Will private marital messages remain private? Will either spouse forward an argument to family members? Will screenshots of a disagreement be shared in order to gather support? Family WhatsApp groups can be useful for sharing news, photographs, invitations, and practical information. They should not become a courtroom for marital disagreements. Agree that ordinary disagreements will first be discussed between the two spouses. If advice is genuinely needed, agree together who to ask, and what to share. Do not circulate screenshots to collect supporters, and do not let a relative's comment in a group become the basis for an argument at home.

If someone makes an inappropriate comment in a group, the couple should discuss how to respond. Sometimes a brief, calm reply is enough: "We appreciate everyone's concern. We are discussing this together and will let you know when we have decided." There is no need to explain every private detail to an audience, and there is no need to respond to every message immediately. A couple can set simple boundaries — for example, that they will not discuss marital disagreements in a family group, that they will mute groups during work hours, and that they will not forward private messages without the other person's knowledge. Boundaries of this kind are not secrecy. They are a way of protecting the private space a marriage needs.

Long-distance in-laws

Diaspora marriages often involve parents living in another country. A husband may work in New York while his parents live in Pakistan. His wife may have her own family in Bangladesh. Distance does not necessarily reduce family involvement. Parents may expect daily calls. They may want to be consulted about household decisions. They may be planning a visit that will last for several weeks. Discuss how you will manage communication. How often will you call? Will both spouses join every family call? Will either person be expected to be available at all hours? What happens when one spouse wants to travel to see their parents and the other cannot travel because of work or visa conditions? These matters should be handled with consideration, not guilt. A couple can remain connected to family without being available every hour of the day, and a person can love their parents deeply while still needing an evening to themselves.

Agree on a review date

A housing decision made before marriage should be revisited when circumstances require it. If you decide to live with parents initially, agree on a date to review the arrangement. If you rent separately, review whether the financial arrangement remains sustainable. If a parent develops additional care needs, discuss how responsibilities might change. A review is not an admission that the original decision failed. It is a way of responding to real life, which rarely stays exactly as it was on the wedding day. Choose an actual date rather than saying, "We will discuss it sometime." At the review, ask whether the arrangement is still affordable, whether each person has sufficient privacy, whether family responsibilities are being managed fairly, whether either spouse has become overwhelmed, and what practical changes are possible. If one person is unhappy, the discussion should not immediately become an argument about loyalty or respect. Listen to the reason first, and then look for a change that addresses it.

A home is more than an address

You may live in a rented flat, a family house, an apartment near the parents, or a home in another country. The address matters, but it does not tell the whole story. A good home needs consideration, privacy, responsibility, and a sense of belonging. Parents should be treated with respect. A spouse should be treated with dignity. Family relationships need time, patience, and clear communication. There will be compromises. Some arrangements will require patience. Others may need to change as the family grows or as parents grow older. Do not enter marriage assuming that the other person will eventually accept whatever arrangement you have chosen for them. Discuss the home before you move into it, and keep discussing it as life changes.

Try this: Draw two possible household plans together on one sheet of paper: your preferred initial arrangement, and a realistic alternative if the first becomes difficult. For each plan, write down the monthly cost, who will live there, how privacy will work, how household work will be shared, and the date on which you will review it. Keep the sheet where you can both see it, and return to it on the review date you have agreed.

Readiness check

  1. Can we each describe, in practical detail, the home we have agreed to — not only the address, but the privacy, the costs, and the daily routine?
  2. Where are we still stuck — space, money, parents' needs, or privacy — and what is making that part hard to settle?
  3. What will we decide next, and by which review date will we look at it again together?
